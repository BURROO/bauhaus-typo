Claiming It Back: Hyper-Femininity as Strategy

In recent years, blonde women have increasingly begun to reclaim the stereotypes historically used against them. Rather than rejecting hyper-femininity, many now lean into it, using exaggeration and performance as tools of self-definition. As a result, the figure of the “dumb blonde” reveals itself as what it has always been: an illusion — an idealized fabrication created through a male gaze.

This shift is particularly visible in the rise of BimboTok, a TikTok subculture that reclaims the word “bimbo” on its own terms. Under the hashtag, which has amassed over 405 million views, creators appear in pink fur stilettos, rhinestones, and bleached hair, delivering deliberately ditzy voiceovers filled with irony and sarcasm. “Hi everyone, it’s Chrissy, your favorite bimbo with the horrible math skills, here to tell you how to be the best bimbo you can be,” declares Chrissy Chlapecka, widely considered the movement’s unofficial poster child. Among her satirical instructions, she concludes with a pointed message to those who underestimate her: “Create a bimbo manifesto and dismantle the patriarchy.”

The cultural groundwork for reclaiming blonde hyper-femininity was laid long before TikTok. Madonna framed blonde sexuality as an empowering force during her Blonde Ambition tour and celebrated Gentlemen Prefer Blondes in her Material Girl video. Legally Blonde offers one of the clearest cinematic rejections of anti-blonde prejudice — so much so that it could reasonably be titled Revenge of the Dumb Blonde.

Historically, the word “bimbo” has been used to ridicule women who were blonde, conventionally attractive, and perceived as unintelligent. Figures such as Dolly Parton, Megan Fox, Jessica Simpson, and Lindsay Lohan were all dismissed through this label. The term itself originates from an Italian masculine noun meaning “baby” and was originally used to describe foolish men — making “himbos” the original bimbos. Over time, actresses like Jean Harlow and Marilyn Monroe solidified the visual shorthand now associated with the stereotype: platinum blonde hair, curvaceous bodies, exaggerated sexuality, and supposed intellectual emptiness.

The contemporary “New-Age Bimbo,” however, reframes this image entirely. Described as kind, politically aware, anti-racist, pro-LGBTQ+, and unapologetically feminine, the new-age bimbo embraces pink, Juicy Couture, and rhinestones alongside self-confidence and ideological clarity. As TikTok user Chrissy Chlapecka defines it, a bimbo is “a radical leftist, who’s pro-sex work, pro-BLM, pro-LGBTQ, pro-choice and will always be there for her girls, gays and theys.” She adds: “I don’t do this for the misogynistic male-gaze, I do this for my gaze! And damn my tits look good.”

The popularity of this trend is inseparable from its inclusivity. Bimbos, himbos, and thembos coexist across gender identities, aligning the movement closely with Generation Z’s intersectional values. TikTok user verydumblonde (Gwyn), who describes herself as a “local communist bimbo,” combines fashion content with humor and discussions of communist theory, further undermining the assumption that femininity and intellect are mutually exclusive.

Ultimately, the resurgence of the bimbo exposes a long-standing cultural bias: femininity itself has been treated as a sign of stupidity. What once served as a punchline is now a strategy. Blondes may have long been reduced to jokes, but in reclaiming the stereotype, they appear to be having the last laugh.

Origins of the “Dumb Blonde”

The precise moment that ignited the “dumb blonde” stereotype remains difficult to pinpoint. As noted in POPSUGAR, even feminist theory historians admit that its exact origin is uncertain. Some trace it to Marilyn Monroe 1953 film Gentlemen Prefer Blondes, in which she famously declares, “I can be smart when it’s important, but most men don’t like it.” Others argue that the association stretches much further back in history, to a time when blonde hair was linked to prostitution in the early Roman Empire. During this period, prostitutes were said to wear blonde wigs or dye their hair blonde, while figures such as Cleopatra — reportedly a brunette — were perceived as powerful and exceptionally well-educated.

According to Victoria Sherrow’s Encyclopedia of Hair, there are clear historical precedents connecting blondness to promiscuity. In the Roman Empire, blonde hair functioned as a visual marker of sexual availability, and this perception persisted in Europe well into the 1300s. Sherrow writes that blonde hair was widely seen as provocative and that artists frequently depicted Eve, the archetypal sinful seductress, as blonde, while the Virgin Mary was portrayed as a brunette. Sherrow also outlines broader cultural reasons for the desirability of blonde hair: as a recessive genetic trait, it was less common than dark hair and therefore more sought after. Its association with gold and light — symbols of value and purity — further enhanced its appeal. Blondness was also linked to youth, a quality historically prized in women as a signifier of fertility.

While these associations established blondness as desirable and erotic, historians largely agree that the explicit connection between blondness and stupidity emerged later. Many trace it back roughly 250 years to the French play Les Curiosités de la Foire, inspired by the life of the courtesan Rosalie Duthé. Duthé was known for her long pauses before speaking, which led audiences to interpret her silence as a lack of intelligence. As a result, blondes became culturally coded as both sexually available and intellectually inferior.

This framework was solidified in twentieth-century popular culture. When Gentlemen Prefer Blondes reached cinemas in 1953, Marilyn Monroe’s portrayal of Lorelei crystallized the stereotype for modern audiences. Lorelei is characterized as absent-minded, slightly scatterbrained, and primarily interested in marrying for money. Her most famous line — “I can be smart when it’s important, but most men don’t like it” — reinforces the idea that female intelligence is both undesirable and expendable. In contrast, her brunette counterpart Dorothy is presented as sharper, more capable, and more grounded. Through this juxtaposition, the dumb blonde stereotype is not only performed but normalized, embedding it firmly into popular consciousness (Refinery29).

Pop Culture, Hollywood, and the “Dumb Blonde”

“Film and pop culture play a significant role in how people view the world in general,” Christie Ferrari, Psy.D., a clinical psychologist, explains in POPSUGAR. “When it relates to women, stereotypes are used to help better tell a story; yet, the problem with stereotypes is that they can be wrong, and the public can generalize them to a whole group of people with similar features and attributes, in this case blondes, and that can affect how people are treated and feel about a group of people.” As Ferrari further notes, “When it pertains to blondes, they are not typically represented and casted as intelligent.”

This lack of representation is neither subtle nor incidental. Popular culture repeatedly draws on the same visual and narrative shorthand, producing a long line of blond female characters whose intelligence is questioned, mocked, or deliberately sidelined. Figures such as Cher Horowitz, Karen Smith, Elle Woods, and Tanya McQuoid circulate through film and television as recognizable variations of the same trope. Ferrari points out that “even with a quick Google search, you’ll notice that Lisa Simpson is one of the few blondes, albeit a cartoon character, that was given the role as intelligent in media,” underscoring how rare such portrayals remain.

These representations are deeply rooted in classical Hollywood typologies. In The Women’s Companion, Annette Kuhn outlines three recurring blond archetypes: the “ice-cold blonde,” the “blonde bombshell,” and the “dumb blonde.” The ice-cold blonde—often associated with Hitchcock—appears controlled and refined, yet carries an implicit sense of deception, suggesting that her innocence cannot be trusted. The blonde bombshell, by contrast, is defined by overt sexuality, her desirability framed as explosive and accessible, while simultaneously positioning her as a commodity—“a prize to be bought.” The dumb blonde unites hypersexuality with ignorance, characterized by an apparent inability to understand even the most basic facts of everyday life (Kuhn).

Although these archetypes differ in tone, they share a common function. All three reduce the blonde woman to an object: idolized, desired, and yet persistently framed as less-than-human. Blondness becomes a visual marker onto which fantasies of danger, desire, and stupidity are projected, flattening individuality in favor of recognizability.

A contemporary extension of the “dumb blonde” stereotype can be found in the public persona of Paris Hilton. Unlike many fictional characters, Hilton’s image was not simply imposed on her but consciously constructed. She created a version of herself that embodied the familiar figure of the blonde, slightly naïve heiress the public came to recognize. Hilton explains that this persona emerged during her time in solitary confinement at Provo Canyon. “I literally thought about who I wanted to be and who I would become,” she recalls. “I started closing my eyes and dreaming of this world: I will work so hard and be so successful that no one will ever control me again. I was inspired by Marilyn Monroe, Barbie, and Dolly Parton — all these blonde icons who definitely played characters. And that helped me not think about the bad things. It was like a total escape.” The “dumb blonde” thus appears not only as a stereotype, but also as a form of self-protection and imagination — a character created to survive and to regain control.

Over time, the boundary between performance and identity began to blur. “I think I also got stuck in that role. I lost myself in the role and forgot who I was before I was sent to Provo Canyon,” Paris Hilton admits. What started as a strategic character slowly became inseparable from her public self. At the same time, this role proved extraordinarily effective. While it reduced Hilton to a stereotype, it also turned her into a globally recognizable figure. The “dumb blonde” persona generated enormous economic value, contributing to a personal fortune estimated at around 300 million US dollars (Glamour). Hilton’s case reveals the contradiction at the core of the stereotype: it is limiting and dehumanizing, yet it can also be deliberately performed, circulated, and monetized.

Rather than breaking with the stereotype, Paris Hilton exposes its mechanics. By playing the role to excess, she demonstrates how deeply the “dumb blonde” is embedded in pop culture — and how easily it can be consumed, repeated, and rewarded.
